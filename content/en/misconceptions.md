Most of what is said about Islam is not a flaw in the religion; it is a flaw in method: a sentence lifted out of its place, an incident judged without its setting, a term read in the wrong language. The way to answer a claim is not to argue louder but to return to the source. What does the text actually say? When was it said? And in what sense is the term being used? Read the original before you pass it on.

:::Ayah text="فَاسْأَلُوا أَهْلَ الذِّكْرِ إِن كُنتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ" ref="النحل — ٤٣" tr="Ask the people of remembrance if you do not know." note="Questioning those who know comes before judging without evidence."
:::

:::Section title="Method: read the source before you repeat it"
:::Cards cols="3"
:::Card icon="📜" title="The source text"
Do not pass on a claim about Islam until you have read where it sits in the Qur’an or the Sunnah. The tools of verification separate sound wording from weak, and the text from what people merely said about it.
:::
:::Card icon="🔍" title="The context"
A verse is read with what comes before and after it, and a ruling with its setting and its time. Specifying a general statement and limiting an open one are part of the legal method, not a breach of it.
:::
:::Card icon="🗂️" title="The term"
Terms such as jihad, Sharia and tawhid have a defined meaning in the vocabulary of the law, and they are not read with whatever weight they carry in some other language.
:::

:::Section title="Eight questions people ask often"
:::FAQ
:::QA q="Is Islam a religion of peace or of harshness?"
The Qur’an describes His messenger: “We have not sent you except as a mercy to the worlds” (al-Anbiya 107), and it commands gentleness in invitation: “Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction” (al-Nahl 125). Harshness seen in some individuals is judged as their action, not as their religion: a person answers for what they do, and a faith is not measured by the conduct of whoever departs from it.
:::
:::QA q="Is the hijab imposed by force?"
Its ruling is stated in the text: “and draw their coverings over their chests” (al-Nur 31), and men are addressed first: “lower their gaze” (al-Nur 30). As for force, the religion itself rejects it: “there is no compulsion in religion” (al-Baqara 256). The right is conveyed by wisdom and good conduct; whoever is coerced into obedience has been opposed to the spirit of the religion itself.
:::
:::QA q="Did Islam spread by the sword?"
The text is explicit that entry into the faith is voluntary: “there is no compulsion in religion; the right course has become clear from the wrong” (al-Baqara 256). The early expansions had a defined policy toward local people: whoever believed entered the faith, and whoever did not remained under covenant, with their religion and their property protected — much as the charters of Medina governed relations with the people of the book. The wide spread of later centuries came through trade, invitation and culture rather than through armies.
:::
:::QA q="Is Sharia too harsh for modern times?"
Sharia is read through its objectives: protecting religion, life, mind, lineage and property, and building law on ease — “Allah intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship” (al-Baqara 185). Renewing means and revising particular rulings is possible and legitimate within the method set by the scholars; what is fixed in the principles serves the age rather than limiting it.
:::
:::QA q="Are women’s rights incomplete?"
Her dignity is an original, settled matter: “The believers, men and women, are allies of one another” (al-Tawbah 71). She holds her own rights to education, property, inheritance, choosing a spouse and motherhood, alongside protection and shared duties. The scale used to measure her today is usually a set of changing civil laws; the texts are firmer, older and wider.
:::
:::QA q="Is jihad killing without criteria?"
Islam set explicit limits: “fight in the way of Allah those who fight you, but do not transgress” (al-Baqara 190), so aggression is forbidden even in war. The killing of women, children and the elderly was never permitted, nor the cutting of trees, the breaking of covenants or harm to protected communities; whoever does these has opposed the prophetic way rather than fulfilled it.
:::
:::QA q="Does trust in God mean doing nothing?"
Tawhid is relying on the heart upon God while taking the means that produce the effect: “prepare against them whatever strength you can” (al-Anfal 60), together with “rely on the Ever-Living who does not die” (al-Furqan 58). Whoever abandons means while claiming trust has kept the words and dropped the condition; whoever relies on means alone has forgotten who holds them. The middle course is the one commanded.
:::
:::QA q="Is Islam a religion that cannot change?"
The fixed principles — oneness, justice and mercy — come from revelation, and they do not change because they are ends rather than instruments. Particular rulings are revisited as times change through the method the scholars built. The Qur’an itself opens the door to reflection: “Do they not contemplate the Qur’an, or are there locks upon their hearts?” (Muhammad 24); what never changes are the limits the Lawgiver set to protect those objectives.
:::
